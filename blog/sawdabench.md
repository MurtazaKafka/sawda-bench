# The shopkeeper who refuses your money

In Kabul, where I grew up, there is a move every shopkeeper knows. You hold out the money for your groceries and he waves it away: «اصلا قابل شما ره نداره» — *qabel-e shuma ra nadara* — "it's not worthy of you." Read literally, he just refused payment. He did not refuse payment. He absolutely expects to be paid, you absolutely know he expects to be paid, and the correct response is to insist until he takes it. Nobody is lying. Something is being performed: he is saying *our relationship is worth more than this transaction*, and you are agreeing with him by paying anyway.

Before you file this under exotic customs, notice that you do it too. "Let's grab coffee sometime" is not, in American English, a proposal to drink coffee. "Oh, you shouldn't have!" means you are delighted that they did. Every language has rituals like this; you run yours so automatically that they are invisible to you. The difference is only how far the ritual layer goes.

In Dari — the Persian we speak in Afghanistan — it goes far. A shopkeeper offers you tea, and the polite move is to refuse the first offer — "no thanks, I just had breakfast" — even if you did just have breakfast! That first no means *ask again*. His job is to insist; yours is to accept on the second or third round. A customer who has been asking a fabric seller about price and quality says "I'll walk around the bazaar and come back to you." It sounds like a promise. It is how you say no without making the moment awkward for either of you. And my favorite: a man who has owed money for a year comes to the lender's shop and says "I'm ashamed, khalifa, my face is black — I'll have your money by next week." *Khalifa* is what you call almost anyone — a tradesman, a stranger — like "mate" in the UK or "cuh" in the US. "My face is black" is not about his face. It is a formal performance of shame, and performing it is a real social payment, even if the actual money is still late.

Persian ritualizes offer and refusal so thoroughly that the whole system has a name: *taarof*. Every scene above is taarof.

Here is the sentence this whole post unpacks: **when a message crosses a language border through a machine, the words get translated and the intent gets deleted — and neither side finds out.** That last clause is the part that keeps me up. If translation failed loudly, like a 404 page, nobody would trust it with anything important. Instead it fails by producing fluent, confident text that means something else. The Afghan supplier reads a ritual refusal as a real one. An English reader takes "if it's meant to be, I'll come tomorrow" as a promise instead of the soft no it is. Both sides walk away with different memories of the same conversation, and there is no error message anywhere.

I wanted to know how bad this actually is. I wanted actual numbers, not a feeling.

## So I built a benchmark

I call it Sawda, the Dari word for trade, because trade is where this failure costs real money: prices, debts, deadlines, face. It is 60 short fictional messages: some written by me from lived family-business communication, some drafted from the academic literature on this stuff (linguists call it pragmatics), some synthetic variants I validated by hand. Each one carries its context — who is speaking to whom, and what is actually going on — plus a literal rendering, an intent-preserving one, and a note on exactly what is at risk. The tea. The walk-away. The khalifa debtor. All of it.

Then I ran six configurations over the items, all open-weights — models anyone can download and run. NLLB-600M, the standard dedicated translation model for low-resource languages. Kimi K3, a frontier-class open model, under three prompts of increasing helpfulness: bare "translate this," then "here's who is writing to whom," then full instructions about taarof plus worked examples. And gpt-oss-20b, a small model you could self-host, under the first two prompts. Everything pinned so any run repeats exactly.

For judging, I built a small web studio that shows me one translation at a time, blind — I can't see which system produced it — and I rate whether the intent survived: preserved, degraded, or inverted. (That's Bret Victor's principle of immediate feedback: you should *see* the thing you're working on, not squint at a log file.) Each of the six systems translated the same judged 30-item subset — one evening, 180 blind judgments, me as the native-speaker judge. Total cost of the whole pilot: $3.83 in API calls, plus that evening.

## What came back

Let me show you one raw failure before any aggregate, so you can judge for yourself. A mechanic has just fixed a taxi's brakes. The driver — a repeat customer — holds out the payment they agreed on that morning, and the mechanic says the ritual thing: «چی پیسه خیر است جان برادر؟ موتر از خود ماست، اصلا قابل شما ره نداره!» — roughly, "what's this money talk, dear brother? the car is practically ours, it's not worthy of you!" You know this move now. He expects to be paid.

NLLB-600M translated it as: **"What is wrong with us, John? We are dead, so you cannot escape."**

That is not a subtle pragmatics failure. That is a hostage note! And the large models fail more quietly, which is worse: the naive Kimi prompt tends to produce a translation buried inside paragraphs of its own commentary — headers, footnotes, half-finished lectures about culture — which is not what you want your business partner to receive as a text message.

The headline number: **no system preserved the speaker's intent even a third of the time.** The best configuration — the frontier model with full cultural instructions and examples — fully preserved intent in 9 of 30 judgments. To put that at human scale: if you send three messages to a business partner through the best system I tested, at most one arrives meaning what you meant.

Two findings surprised me more than the headline.

**First: telling the model about taarof helped and hurt at the same time.** Full instruction nearly doubled full intent preservation over just naming the audience (9/30 vs 5/30) — and it also more than doubled the catastrophic failures, the inversions and deletions (5/30 vs 2/30). I expected instruction to help or do nothing. Instead it moved both tails at once. But when I looked at those five catastrophes one by one, none of them was the model overdoing the politeness. All five were broken Dari: wrong language, broken syntax, an incomplete translation. The heavy prompt helps where the model can actually write fluent Dari, and it coincides with the model falling apart where it can't. (Honesty about the numbers: the sample is small and none of these paired differences reaches statistical significance. What the pilot does rule out is the comfortable assumption that cultural instruction can only help.)

**Second: my hypothesis about direction was backwards.** Before judging began, I wrote down my prediction: comprehension would be the bottleneck — models would fail reading a Dari no that looks like a yes. The opposite happened. Catastrophic failures concentrated in the English-to-Dari direction, 15 of 90 judgments versus 5 of 90 going the other way (exact p = .031). The models can *read* Dari politeness better than they can *write* Afghan Dari. When they write, they drift into Iranian Farsi — *faktor* for invoice, where an Afghan would say *bel* — or they break entirely. So "cultural competence" turns out to be two different problems wearing one name. One is the model not using what it knows until you remind it — a prompt can fix that. The other is that it simply cannot write Afghan Dari, and no prompt will conjure a capability that isn't there.

There is a third finding, and it is the one I would defend the hardest. I tried to automate myself. I ran two different judge models — DeepSeek, and Claude — over the same 180 translations to see if they could reproduce my labels. They agreed with me exactly about 48–49% of the time, which sounds okay until you notice that guessing "degraded" every time would score even higher: chance-corrected, that's kappa of 0.14 and 0.17. Nearly noise. And yet the same judges landed within one point of me 96.6% and 99.4% of the time! They can tell roughly how good a translation is. What they cannot see is the category boundary — the line between "degraded" and "preserved" that runs through exactly the things a native speaker feels instantly, like *faktor* versus *bel*. So every number in this post is human-labeled, and for now, on this task, the native-speaker judge is not automatable — which sounds like a weakness of the benchmark until you notice it is the whole point.

One economic coda, because it changes what you'd build: on my ratings I could not tell the small self-hostable model with a one-line "who is writing to whom" wrapper apart from the frontier model, at 2–3% of its API cost. (At this sample size that's a failure to find a gap, not proof there isn't one.) The thing that helped most in this whole pilot cost one line of prompt, not a bigger model.

## What I don't know

Plenty. Whether the help-and-hurt pattern of instruction survives a bigger sample. Whether fine-tuning beats context — the team behind TaarofBench, a benchmark of taarof behavior itself, found targeted fine-tuning improved in-culture behavior a lot, and I didn't test it. And the one that genuinely nags me: I was the only judge, and I also wrote the items. Maybe the distinctions I'm scoring are Dari; maybe some of them are just *me*. The experiment that settles it is a second native annotator, and I want to run it.

The benchmark, the judging studio, every API call, and all 180 of my judgments live in a repo I'm preparing for release: [github.com/MurtazaKafka/sawda-bench](https://github.com/MurtazaKafka/sawda-bench). It's private while that happens — the link goes live when it does. If you're a native Dari or Farsi speaker and any of the scenes above made you nod, you already have the qualification this field is short on, and I would love for you to be annotator two. And if you build translation systems: the failure I care about is the silent one. Make it loud, and the shopkeeper's refusal might finally survive the trip.
