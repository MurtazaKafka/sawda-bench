# How Feynman Explained Hard Things: Operational Principles

Distilled from primary sources: the BBC *Fun to Imagine* interview (1983, "magnets" segment), the Auckland/Robb lectures published as *QED: The Strange Theory of Light and Matter* (1985, Ch. 1), *Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman!* ("O Americano, Outra Vez!"), and *The Feynman Lectures on Physics* Vol. I Ch. 1 ("Atoms in Motion"). Each principle is a move a writer can execute, with the Feynman evidence attached.

---

## 1. Open with the stakes of a single sentence

Before explaining anything, compress the entire subject into one carrier sentence and tell the reader why that sentence, of all sentences, is the one worth saving. This does two jobs at once: it states the core claim up front, and it signals that everything that follows is *unpacking*, not accumulation. The reader always knows what the details are details *of*.

> "If, in some cataclysm, all of scientific knowledge were to be destroyed, and only one sentence passed on to the next generation of creatures, what statement would contain the most information in the fewest words?" — *Lectures*, Vol. I Ch. 1. His answer is the atomic hypothesis, from which "an enormous amount of information about the world" follows "if just a little imagination and thinking are applied."

**Execution:** write the one sentence a reader should retain if they forget everything else; put it in the first section; make every later section derivable from it.

## 2. Start from an object the reader can already picture, then zoom

Feynman does not begin "Atoms in Motion" with atoms. He begins with a drop of water a quarter of an inch across — smooth, continuous, familiar — and magnifies it in explicit, stated steps: two thousand times (now it's forty feet across, "about as big as a large room," still smooth water), then two thousand times more (now a teeming swarm, like a crowd at a football game seen from far away). The abstraction (molecules) arrives only after the reader has ridden a continuous visual path from their kitchen sink to it. Each zoom step is quantified and re-anchored to a human-scale comparison (a room, a crowd, an apple magnified to the size of the Earth so its atoms become apple-sized).

**Execution:** never introduce an abstraction cold. Pick one concrete instance, describe it at the scale the reader lives at, then move toward the abstraction in explicit steps, restating the human-scale equivalent at each step.

## 3. Name the phenomenon only after it exists in the reader's head

In Brazil, Feynman found students who could recite "Brewster's Angle" — tangent of the angle equals the index of refraction — but could not connect it to sunlight glinting off the bay outside the window. In his farewell lecture he opened the standard textbook at random to "triboluminescence: the light emitted when crystals are crushed" and pointed out that this defines a word with other words. The real version — crush a lump of sugar with pliers in the dark and you'll see a bluish flash — is something a reader can go home and *try*; only then is anything about nature actually communicated. His lifelong formulation (from his father, in *What Do You Care What Other People Think?*): knowing the name of something is not the same as knowing something.

**Execution:** for every technical term you plan to use, first describe the phenomenon in ordinary words — ideally as something the reader could observe or check — and only then attach the label, as a convenience for later reference. If you can't describe the thing without its name, you don't yet have an explanation; you have vocabulary.

## 4. Tell the reader honestly what they won't understand — and why that's fine

Feynman opens the QED lectures by *lowering* the comprehension bar rather than pretending the material is easy: "you're not going to be able to understand it... It is my task to convince you not to turn away because you don't understand it. You see, my physics students don't understand it either. That is because *I* don't understand it. Nobody does." Then he makes the crucial distinction that rescues the reader: "while I am describing to you *how* Nature works, you won't understand *why* Nature works that way. But you see, nobody understands that." The how is fully knowable; the why is open — and confusing the two is what makes readers feel stupid and quit. Elsewhere he tells audiences: "do not take the lecture too seriously... just relax and enjoy it."

**Execution:** at the top of a technical piece, separate what the reader will genuinely get (the how: what was done, what was measured) from what remains genuinely open (the why), and say so plainly. Honest incompleteness builds more trust than false completeness, and it converts confusion from a failure state into the expected, shared condition.

## 5. Use analogies as load-bearing structures — and refuse the ones that are circular

In the magnets interview, Feynman refuses the obvious move: "if I said the magnets attract like as if they were connected by rubber bands, I would be cheating you," because rubber bands' elasticity is *itself* caused by electrical forces — he'd be using the mystery to explain the mystery, and a curious reader would soon "ask me about the nature of the band." His conclusion is stark honesty: "I can't explain that attraction in terms of anything else that is familiar to you." Contrast this with analogies he *does* use (the football-crowd for molecular jostling, the LA-to-New-York hair's-width for precision): those carry real structure from the familiar domain to the new one and don't smuggle the conclusion in.

**Execution:** before deploying an analogy, check two things — (a) does the mapping actually carry the mechanism, or just the vibe? and (b) does the familiar side secretly depend on the thing being explained? Then state explicitly where the analogy breaks. An analogy whose failure mode is unmarked is a debt the reader pays later.

## 6. Know what framework your "why" bottoms out in

The deepest lesson of the magnets interview: "why" questions only have answers relative to a framework the audience already accepts. Feynman's example is Aunt Minnie in the hospital — "because she slipped on the ice and broke her hip" satisfies most people, but each answer spawns another why (why is ice slippery?), and "if you try to follow anything up, you go deeper and deeper in various directions." A good explainer chooses, deliberately, the level at which to stop — the level of things the audience will "take as understood" — and says when something must simply be accepted as a starting fact rather than faking a deeper reduction.

**Execution:** decide before writing which facts you will treat as bedrock for this audience, state them as givens without apology, and answer why-questions only down to that level. When a reader's natural next "why" goes below your floor, acknowledge the floor explicitly instead of hand-waving past it.

## 7. Make the reader's own experience the laboratory

Feynman constantly closes the loop between claim and something the reader can verify unaided: blow on hot soup and it cools (fast molecules escape first); crush sugar in the dark and see the flash; look at the glare off water through a polarizing lens and turn your head. In *Fun to Imagine* the whole program is structured around household objects — rubber bands, mirrors, fire — reinterpreted through jiggling atoms. The claim earns belief because the evidence is already in the reader's possession.

**Execution:** for each key claim, ask: what has the reader already seen — or could see in two minutes — that this claim predicts or reinterprets? Put that observable next to the claim. In empirical writing, this also means showing raw examples the reader can judge for themselves, not just aggregates they must take on faith.

## 8. Calibrate numbers against the human body and the map

Feynman never leaves a precision claim as a bare number. QED's agreement with experiment — theory 1.00115965246 vs. experiment 1.00115965221 — is immediately translated: "If you were to measure the distance from Los Angeles to New York to this accuracy, it would be exact to the thickness of a human hair." And he's candid about the rhetorical function: "These numbers are meant to intimidate you into believing that the theory is probably not too far off!" The move has two parts — a physical re-scaling the body can feel, and transparency about what the number is *for* in the argument.

**Execution:** every load-bearing quantity gets a companion image at human scale (a hair, a room, a football crowd), and you say outright what the number is supposed to make the reader believe. Never let a decimal do persuasive work silently.

## 9. Flag your simplifications as simplifications — don't secretly lie

Feynman simplifies constantly, but he labels the seams. After sketching how physics developed, he interrupts himself: "what I have just outlined is what I call a 'physicist's history of physics,' which is never correct. What I am telling you is a sort of conventionalized myth-story... not necessarily related to the actual historical development, which I do not really know!" Likewise he warns that physicists "use ordinary words such as 'work' or 'action' or 'energy'... for some technical purpose," and that he may slip into that usage without noticing. The simplification stays useful because the reader is told its epistemic status.

**Execution:** when you idealize, average, or tell a cleaned-up story, say in the text that you're doing it and roughly what got cleaned away. Separately, watch for ordinary words your field has quietly redefined — either avoid them or flag the collision at first use.

## 10. Prefer explaining one thing you know cold over surveying ten you half-know

Feynman opens QED by refusing the audience's default appetite: "People are always asking for the latest developments in the unification of this theory with that theory... They always want to know things that we don't know. So, rather than confound you with a lot of half-cooked, partially analyzed theories, I would like to tell you about a subject that has been very thoroughly analyzed." Depth on solid ground beats breadth on mush — and it's also the Feynman-technique test in reverse: if you can't take the reader all the way down on a topic, that's a gap in *your* understanding, and the writing will show it.

**Execution:** scope the piece to the claims you can defend down to bedrock (principle 6). Cut or explicitly quarantine the speculative material ("here's what we don't know yet") rather than blending known and half-known at the same rhetorical confidence.
